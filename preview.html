True Ministry

I remember listening to a minister share an experience that has stayed with me for sometime. He was traveling to attend a Christian function in a town he had never visited before. Not knowing the exact location, he felt slightly anxious about finding his way. As he walked, he noticed a young lady ahead of him. There was nothing extravagant about her — no Bible in her hand, no badge announcing where she was going. But something about her composure, her modesty, her quiet confidence spoke differently. Almost instinctively, he thought to himself, “She must be one of the believers.”

Curious, he decided to follow at a distance, and to his amazement, the lady was indeed walking to the very same occasion he was searching for. Later, as he recounted the story, he admitted how humbled he was: “Without speaking a word, without introducing herself, this young lady’s life silently testified of her faith. She carried an identity that could be seen, not just claimed.”

True ministry is not first identified by our lips but by our lives. The world notices what we radiate before it hears what we proclaim. Jesus said, “By this everyone will know that you are my disciples...”. Sometimes authenticity in discipleship is not in preaching loud sermons, but in walking quietly with Christ until His presence is unmistakable in us.

I have walked through different circles in my life — chat rooms, office corridors, market stalls, even online forums — and I've observed something consistent: people aren’t mostly interested in whether we know all the right doctrines, but whether we live those doctrines. They are watching not from pews or pulpits only, but from lives intersecting with ours. It’s the alignment between what we say we believe and how we behave that becomes the proof of our faith. The world looks for lived-out conviction more than polished arguments.

Scripture underscores this deeply. James says, “Faith by itself, if it does not have works, is dead” (James 2:17). “...Faith without works is a dead faith because the lack of works reveals an unchanged life or a spiritually dead heart. There are many verses that say that true saving faith will result in a transformed life, that faith is demonstrated by the works we do. How we live reveals what we believe and whether the faith we profess to have is a living faith.” (GotQuestions.org) It’s not that doctrine is unimportant, far from it. But doctrine disconnected from life is like a map never used. True faith shows itself not only in what we teach, but in how we treat people, how we respond under pressure, how we keep promises. The believer’s life becomes the sermon.

An article from GotQuestions explains that “real saving faith is demonstrated by good works” and that “faith without works is dead faith because the lack of works reveals an unchanged life or a spiritually dead heart.” Likewise, Theology of Work argues that integrity is “a life that hangs together” — a wholeness of belief, word, and behavior — and that people are drawn to God by the integrity of our lives.

So when I say that discipleship (or authenticity, or integrity) is not self-certified but verified, I mean this: there are unseen eyes watching — neighbors, co‐workers, family members, even strangers. They note when what is preached is not practiced. They see if mercy is shown when anger would have been easier, if honesty is kept when deception seems profitable, if love is offered when rejection would be safer. The credibility of our discipleship is not in what we claim for ourselves, but in what others see in us when we don’t even know they are looking.

The early church understood this truth deeply. Scripture tells us in Acts 4:13 that when the council witnessed the boldness of Peter and John, they “realized that they were unschooled, ordinary men; they were astonished, and they took note that these men had been with Jesus.” Their influence did not come from theological seminaries, academic scrolls, or societal rank but from something far more compelling: the visible evidence of Christ’s presence in their lives.

I recall hearing of a simpler example from some motivational speaker: a man once shared how, on his first day at a new job, he observed a colleague who, under pressure, spoke gently, offered help without being asked, and refused to join in unnecessary conversations. That colleague had no banner, no title, yet by the end of two months everyone recognized that something was different in him. People began to trust his word, because his life bore witness. That is what happens when someone has truly ‘been with Jesus’ — their life testifies even when their lips are quiet.

“Christianity, as Ellen White saw it, is to affect every part of a person’s daily life. Far from being something that happens to people when they are in church, true Christianity transforms people from the inside out. It changes their hearts, but that inner change, if it is genuine, carries over into family relationships, schooling and work, and even how people use their spare time.” (From Practical Christianity and the Development of Christian Character, Ellen G. White Writings) In other words, what people note is not our diploma or our title but the consistency of our humble, godly behavior in everyday situations. That is the heart of authentic Christian influence — not measured by credentials, but by character visible to all.

But let me confess something to you: living a life that speaks Christ even when I am silent has never been easy. There have been moments when anger rose in me, and I longed to release sharp words. There were times when shortcuts appeared so tempting, when compromising just a little seemed harmless. In those quiet struggles, I realized that discipleship is often proven not on the stage of public ministry, but in the hidden places of daily life, where decisions are made in secret yet echo loudly in character.

In many ways, I have felt like Paul when he wrote, “I beat my div and make it my slave so that after I have preached to others, I myself will not be disqualified” (1 Corinthians 9:27). This verse resonates with me because my discipleship is constantly tested not only when I stand in church but also when I buy in the market, when I speak with colleagues, when I face disappointments, and even when I am alone. True discipleship is not a Sabbath garment but a daily walk. It is in these ordinary dealings of life that the authenticity of my faith is weighed and its credibility either strengthened or weakened.

The words of Ellen G. White capture this truth with piercing clarity: “The greatest evidence of the power of Christianity that can be presented to the world is a well-ordered, well-disciplined family. This will recommend the truth as nothing else can, for it is a living witness of its practical power upon the heart” (The Adventist Home). In other words, the power of the gospel is not confined to eloquent sermons or public declarations, but it is most convincingly seen in the ordinary rhythms of life. It is revealed in the atmosphere of the home, where kindness and patience prevail even in moments of tension. It shines in the marketplace, where honesty and fairness guide every transaction, even when dishonesty could yield greater profit. It speaks loudly in how one responds to insults, with gentleness rather than retaliation. Such everyday demonstrations of Christian character become sermons without words, proving that discipleship is not a performance for the pulpit but a witness in every arena of life.

I have realized that credibility in discipleship grows through consistency. A disciple who is dependable, who prays today and prays tomorrow, who loves today and forgives tomorrow, who stands firm in adversity — such a disciple becomes like a city on a hill that cannot be hidden (Matthew 5:14). People may not remember all our sermons, but they will never forget the authenticity of our lives.

Think of Joseph in Egypt. His credibility was tested in the darkness of Potiphar’s house, in the silence of a prison cell, and later in the brightness of Pharaoh’s palace. Through it all, his discipleship was not identified by self-proclamation but by the witness of those around him: “The LORD was with Joseph and gave him success in whatever he did” (Genesis 39:23). Even pagans could testify that there was something divine in his conduct.

Friends, discipleship is not a mere title we wear, it is a living testimony that speaks louder than words. It is not a badge pinned to our chest, but a life consistently surrendered to Christ. Just as a coin has no value without currency, so discipleship without credibility has no weight before the watching world. When our words align with our deeds, when love flows without prejudice, and when humility is not a show but a genuine posture of the heart, then even skeptics begin to take notice. A life lived with integrity becomes a sermon more persuasive than the most eloquent speech. Think of salt — it does not announce itself, yet it changes everything it touches. So too, the credibility of a disciple seasons the world with the flavor of Christ’s presence.

Jesus Himself prayed in John 17:21 that the world would believe in Him through the unity and authenticity of His followers. That prayer reminds us that our credibility is never just about us, it carries eternal consequences. It is not merely about preserving our own reputation, but about becoming instruments through which others may come to faith. When the world sees Christians loving across divisions, forgiving when wronged, and standing together in humility, the gospel becomes believable. In that sense, our everyday choices, whether at work, at home, or in public — are not private matters alone. They are part of God’s mission to save others through the living testimony of His people. Our credibility, then, is holy ground, for through it, the light of Christ breaks into a darkened world.

Again I will not pretend that I always measure up perfectly. Like Peter, there are moments I have denied Christ, not always with words but sometimes with actions. Yet, I also know that credibility is not destroyed by failure but by refusing to repent. When Peter wept bitterly and returned to the Lord, his credibility was restored and strengthened. In fact, his later life bore such convincing power that even his shadow was sought for healing (Acts 5:15).

Credibility in discipleship is not optional; it is the silent sermon that accompanies every word we speak. It is the fragrance that lingers long after we have left a room, the unseen witness that follows us into classrooms, offices, homes, and marketplaces. Just as the aroma of a flower cannot be hidden, the authenticity of a disciple naturally spreads, whether for good or ill. The truest measure of faith is not what is proclaimed in the spotlight, but what is observed in the ordinary, unguarded moments of life.

The Apostle Paul captured this truth when he wrote to the Corinthians: “You yourselves are our letter, written on our hearts, known and read by everyone. You show that you are a letter from Christ” (2 Corinthians 3:2–3). Every believer is, in essence, a living letter that communicates Christ to the world. And unlike ink on paper, our lives are read in daily actions — how we love, how we forgive, how we endure trials, and how we treat those who can offer us nothing in return. That is credibility: the undeniable evidence that Christ dwells within us, written in a language the world cannot ignore.

Beloved, the greatest question is not what we claim about ourselves, but what others can unmistakably see in us. Each day, our lives are like open books, silently preaching sermons that may either draw people to Christ or push them away. Let us, therefore, be intentional that the message written in our actions, words, and attitudes testifies that we have indeed been with Jesus. True discipleship does not require constant self-assertion; it requires consistent reflection of Christ’s character until the world itself cannot deny His presence in us. May our light shine so naturally, so beautifully, that even without a spoken word, someone might look at us and say with certainty, “I know you belong to Jesus.”

And when such a testimony arises, may it never point to our own strength, but to the transforming power of God’s grace. For it is He who called us out of darkness, He who saved us through the sacrifice of His Son, and He who patiently molds us day by day into authentic disciples. All glory must return to the One who makes the impossible possible, who turns ordinary people into extraordinary witnesses of His kingdom. May our credibility as disciples shine, not to elevate ourselves, but to magnify the name of Christ, until the day the world sees Him fully revealed in His people.
